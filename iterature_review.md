# Literature Review

Five sources were chosen from the project's 65-article evidence set (Stages 1–6) and from the paper that defined the outcome measure. Each source covers one link in the chain this project studies: economic shock → financial strain and distress → health-cost financing → cardiometabolic risk. Each summary is in my own words.

---

### 1. Lusardi, Schneider & Tufano (2011): *Financially fragile households: Evidence and implications*

The authors asked people whether they could come up with USD 2,000 within a month if an unexpected need arose. They found that about a quarter of Americans certainly could not, and close to half were fragile once those who "probably" could not were counted. Fragility was worst among less-educated adults, families with children and people who had lost wealth. Savings came first in a "pecking order" of coping strategies, followed by family help, credit and selling possessions. **Relevance:** this question is the direct ancestor of Findex items `fin24`/`fin24a`, which define the FRAGILE target here.

### 2. Leive & Xu (2008): *Coping with out-of-pocket health payments: empirical evidence from 15 African countries*

Using World Health Survey data, this study found that between about a quarter and two-thirds of households paying for health care financed it by borrowing or selling assets. High inpatient spending raised the odds of such "hardship financing" in most countries, while the richest households were usually protected. **Relevance:** it shows that health shocks travel into household debt. That justifies the secondary target (borrowing for medical purposes) and the use of income quintile and borrowing items as predictors.

### 3. Rahman, Gilmour, Saito, Sultana & Shibuya (2013): *Health-related financial catastrophe, inequality and chronic illness in Bangladesh*

This household survey of Rajshahi city in urban Bangladesh found that 8.6% of households had catastrophic health spending. Risk was several times higher in the poorest quintile, among households with an inpatient admission and in households headed by someone without schooling. Chronic illness raised out-of-pocket costs. **Relevance:** it gives urban-Bangladesh evidence that poverty, education and illness concentrate financial catastrophe. That supports the income and education predictors and the Bangladesh subgroup check.

### 4. Ridley, Rao, Schilbach & Patel (2020): *Poverty, depression, and anxiety: Causal evidence and mechanisms* (Science)

This review of experimental and quasi-experimental studies concludes that poverty and common mental disorders cause each other. Anti-poverty programmes such as cash transfers produce small but real improvements in mental health, and treating depression can improve work and earnings. Economic shocks and uncertainty are named as the channels. **Relevance:** it supports treating financial worry (`fin45`) as a stress marker on the pathway, and it frames social protection (government transfers) as a potential buffer.

### 5. Akter et al. (2014): *Prevalence of diabetes and prediabetes and their risk factors among Bangladeshi adults: a nationwide survey*

Using measured fasting glucose from the 2011 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), this study found that diabetes was common among adults aged 35 and over. It was more common in urban and wealthier groups, and more than half of adults with diabetes did not know they had it. **Relevance:** it marks the cardiometabolic end of the research question that Findex cannot measure. It also explains why the Stage 10–11 plan names BDHS biomarker data as the next dataset for the integrated-care model.

## Synthesis: what the literature implies for this project

* **Outcome definition:** the "emergency funds within 30 days" item is a validated, widely used marker of financial fragility (Sources 1 and 2).
* **Predictors:** income position, education, borrowing and illness-related spending recur as risk markers (Sources 2 and 3). Social protection and stress are plausible buffers and channels (Source 4).
* **Gap:** every study above is inferential and fitted to the whole sample. None reports held-out prediction, calibration or cross-region transfer, and none uses the 2024–25 Findex wave. This project fills that methodological gap for the financial-resilience part of the pathway. It leaves the cardiometabolic part (Source 5) to future work with biomarker data.
